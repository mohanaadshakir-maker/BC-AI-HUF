# BC-AI-HUF: Rebuilt Simulation — Methodology and Results

This document reports a full, from-scratch rebuild of the three BC-AI-HUF
simulation layers (AI/XAI, Blockchain, Human Factor), executed as real code
against real or rigorously modelled data — not narrative numbers. Every
figure below was produced by a script in this project (`ai_layer/`,
`blockchain_layer/`, `human_factor_layer/`) and can be regenerated by
re-running that script.

The goal of this document is twofold: (1) explain exactly how each number
was produced and what it's grounded in, and (2) compare the new, honest
numbers against what the current manuscript claims, so you can see exactly
where the manuscript will need to change.

---

## 1. AI / XAI Layer

### 1.1 Data and setup

Real data: **NSL-KDD** (Tavallaee et al., 2009), the standard, de-duplicated
successor to KDD Cup 1999 — 125,973 training rows, 22,544 test rows, 41 raw
features, one-hot encoded to 122 model features. Train and test come from
UNB's official split and are disjoint by design.

A critical, verified property of this dataset: **17 attack subtypes appear
in the test set that never appear anywhere in training** (apache2,
httptunnel, mailbomb, mscan, named, processtable, ps, saint, sendmail,
snmpgetattack, snmpguess, sqlattack, udpstorm, worm, xlock, xsnoop, xterm —
3,750 of the 22,544 test rows). This is confirmed by direct set comparison
of the label columns, not assumed. It gives a genuine, data-driven zero-day
test for free — no synthetic "held-out attack" needs to be invented.

Two real models were trained on the same data:
- **XGBoost** (300 trees, depth 6) — strong tabular baseline; also the
  model SHAP and LIME explain.
- **AttentionNet** — a small feed-forward network with a learned per-input
  sigmoid attention/gating layer trained jointly with the classifier (so
  the attention weights reflect what the trained model actually leans on,
  not a post-hoc add-on); also the model Integrated Gradients explains.

### 1.2 Core detection metrics (real, held-out KDDTest+)

| Metric | XGBoost | AttentionNet | Original paper claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | 78.8% | 79.2% | 95.8% |
| AUC-ROC | 0.967 | 0.925 | 0.981 |
| False alarm rate | 2.8% | 6.0% | 1.8% |
| Recall (attack detection rate) | 64.9% | 68.0% | not separately stated |
| Detection time / sample | 7.65 ms | 0.074 ms | 3.7 seconds |

**Why accuracy is lower than the original claim, and why that's expected,
not a bug:** NSL-KDD's test set was deliberately constructed to include
attack types absent from training, specifically so that models can't just
memorise the training distribution and score unrealistically high. A ~78–80%
accuracy ceiling on NSL-KDD (rather than 95%+) is a well-documented,
widely-discussed property of this benchmark in the literature — evaluating
on it honestly makes the ~95.8% figure in the manuscript very hard to
defend unless it came from a different, easier evaluation setup (e.g. a
random same-distribution split rather than the official train/test split,
or a different/easier dataset). This is worth resolving before submission:
if the original 95.8% came from a simple random split of a single dataset
(no genuinely novel attacks held out), a reviewer who knows NSL-KDD may
notice the discrepancy.

**Detection time**: 3.7 seconds per sample is not a plausible inference
latency for any of these model classes on any hardware — real single-sample
inference for tree ensembles or small neural nets is single-digit
milliseconds, as measured here. The original figure most likely referred to
something else (e.g. a batch/pipeline stage, or a training time) mislabeled
as per-sample detection time; it should not be reused as-is.

### 1.3 Zero-day / novel-attack generalisation (real, first genuine measurement)

| | Known attacks (seen types) | Novel attacks (never in training) | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| XGBoost detection rate | 77.4% | 34.5% | 42.9 points |
| AttentionNet detection rate | 77.5% | 44.9% | 32.6 points |

This is a substantial, quantified weakness — much larger than the
manuscript's vague "relatively lower" phrasing suggests. Per-attack-type
detail (17 novel types, `results/zero_day_analysis.json`) shows the
weakness isn't uniform: some novel attacks are caught well (saint 99.7%,
apache2 70–82%) while others are almost entirely missed (mailbomb,
snmpguess, snmpgetattack all under 2%). That variation is itself a useful,
honest finding for the paper's Limitations/Discussion.

### 1.4 Interpretability (SHAP, LIME, Attention, Integrated Gradients)

Four **standard, cited metric definitions** were implemented (see
docstring in `ai_layer/xai_evaluate.py` for full citations): Fidelity
(Yeh et al. 2019 infidelity), Stability (Alvarez-Melis & Jaakkola 2018
Lipschitz robustness), Completeness (DeYoung et al. 2020 ERASER
comprehensiveness), and a Complexity proxy (Bhatt et al. 2020) — the last
one explicitly labelled as **not** a substitute for the original paper's
"human intelligibility" rating, which requires real human raters (see
§1.5).

| Method | Fidelity (corr.) | Stability (cosine sim.) | Completeness | Complexity proxy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SHAP | 0.595 | −0.030 | 0.981 | 0.439 |
| LIME | 0.023 | −0.045 | 0.708 | 0.171 |
| Attention | 0.047 | −0.032 | 0.910 | 0.009 |
| Integrated Gradients | 0.379 | 0.137 | 0.976 | 0.438 |
| **Integrated ensemble (mean of the three above)** | **0.850** | −0.003 | 0.948 | 0.070 |

Compare to the manuscript's claimed 0.96 / 0.93 / 0.90 / 4.7 (out of 5),
where the integrated method wins on literally every metric. The real
numbers are messier — which is what real interpretability evaluation looks
like. The integrated ensemble does win decisively on **fidelity
correlation** (0.85 vs the next-best 0.60), which is arguably the most
important of the four properties (faithfulness to what the model actually
does), but it does **not** uniformly dominate the others — e.g. SHAP alone
has marginally higher completeness. Reporting this honestly (integrated
method wins on the primary metric, mixed on secondary ones) is far more
defensible to a reviewer than "wins on everything."

**Important, genuinely useful finding**: the stability metric is
near-zero or slightly negative specifically for the three methods
explaining the tree-based XGBoost model (SHAP, LIME, and the ensemble that
includes SHAP), while it's positive for Integrated Gradients on the
differentiable AttentionNet. This lines up with a well-known critique in
the XAI literature: gradient/Lipschitz-style stability metrics are
naturally suited to differentiable models and behave poorly on tree
ensembles, whose prediction surfaces are piecewise-constant. This is worth
a sentence in the Limitations section — it's a real methodological
insight, not a failure of the analysis.

### 1.5 What could NOT be honestly computed: human intelligibility

The manuscript's "4.7/5 human comprehensibility" figure is, by definition,
a rating from human users, not something derivable from code. Given the
human-participant study was discontinued, there is currently no legitimate
way to report a number here. Two honest options: (a) run a small real
rating study (even 15–20 colleagues/students rating a handful of SHAP vs
LIME vs integrated explanations on a 1–5 Likert scale would be enough for a
legitimate, citable figure — this is a much smaller ask than the full
behavioural study that was discontinued), or (b) drop the human
intelligibility figure from the Results and rely on the computed Fidelity/
Stability/Completeness/Complexity numbers instead, noting explicitly that
comprehensibility awaits a follow-up user study.

## 2. Blockchain Layer

### 2.1 Model

A discrete-event simulation (`blockchain_layer/simulate.py`, built with
`simpy`) of a 5-node private Proof-of-Stake network, block-batched (the
correct unit — many transactions are finalised together by one consensus
round, not one round per transaction). Every parameter is documented and
cited in the script's docstring: 2-second block time, 20–150ms inter-node
network latency (cloud intra/inter-region benchmarks), 2-round BFT-style
voting among 5 validators (quorum = 4, i.e. tolerates 1 faulty node,
matching standard BFT finality-gadget requirements), 5–20ms sequential
per-transaction contract execution (EVM-style single-threaded validation).

**Why not mainnet Ethereum**: mainnet PoS uses a 12-second slot time and
multi-minute finality, incompatible with the framework's sub-3-second
target. What MetaMask + "PoS" + "5 nodes" most plausibly describes is a
private/permissioned PoS testbed (e.g. Geth `--dev` or a custom validator
set), which is what's modelled here.

### 2.2 Results vs manuscript claims

| Metric | Rebuilt simulation | Original paper claim |
|---|---|---|
| Latency without blockchain | 49.9 ms | 45 ms |
| Latency with blockchain (100 TPS load) | 13.6 s (mean), 23.6 s (p95) | 2,800 ms |
| Throughput ceiling (achieved TPS under load) | ~65 TPS | 420 TPS |
| Saturation point (mean latency > 5s) | ~100 TPS offered | 450–500 TPS |
| Availability with blockchain | 99.999% | 99.97% |
| Availability without blockchain (baseline) | 99.9% | not stated |
| Storage growth with blockchain | 2.09 GB/day | 22 GB/day |
| Storage growth without blockchain | 0.56 GB/day | 3 GB/day |

The direction of every effect matches the manuscript (blockchain adds
latency, lowers throughput, raises availability, raises storage growth) —
but the **magnitudes differ substantially**, in both directions. Two honest
findings worth understanding before deciding how to use these numbers:

1. **Throughput ceiling (~65 TPS, not 420 TPS)**: this falls out of a
   well-known real constraint — EVM-style chains validate transactions
   *sequentially* within a block (every node re-executes every transaction
   to verify it), so per-transaction execution cost creates a hard
   throughput ceiling regardless of block size or block time. With 5–20ms
   per transaction, the ceiling lands around 65–80 TPS in this model. This
   is actually closer to real-world reported EVM-chain throughput (mainnet
   Ethereum itself does ~15–30 TPS) than the manuscript's 420 TPS claim,
   which would require either much cheaper per-tx execution or a
   fundamentally different (non-EVM-sequential) execution model — worth
   flagging as a design assumption to revisit if 420 TPS is a hard
   requirement.
2. **Latency being far higher here** follows directly from the lower
   throughput ceiling: once offered load approaches the ~65 TPS ceiling,
   the mempool backs up and mean latency climbs into the tens of seconds.
   This is the simulator correctly reflecting queueing behaviour under a
   sustained-100-TPS load that exceeds its own processing ceiling — it
   is not a bug, it's what "throughput ceiling of 65 TPS" *means* under
   100 TPS of offered load.

If 420 TPS / sub-3-second latency is what was actually observed on real
hardware in the original work, the gap suggests this simulator's per-tx
execution-time assumption (5–20ms) is too conservative for whatever setup
was actually used, and that parameter should be recalibrated against real
benchmark numbers from the actual test environment (e.g. if the real
system parallelises validation across nodes, or uses a lighter-weight
consensus, the sequential-execution ceiling wouldn't apply the same way).
This is flagged explicitly rather than silently tuned to match — see
§4 for the recommended next step.

Statistical validation (15 repeated runs, different seeds): the latency
increase from adding blockchain is consistent and highly significant
(paired t-test, t=160.2, p=3.0×10⁻²⁴).

## 3. Human Factor Layer

### 3.1 Model

An agent-based model (`human_factor_layer/simulate_abm.py`) with 40 agents
per role (Security Analyst, System Administrator, Operator, User; 160
agents total), each following an individually-parameterised exponential
learning curve toward a personal skill asymptote, over 12 weekly steps.
Role-level parameter differences (higher starting skill, higher asymptote,
faster learning for Security Analyst/System Administrator vs
Operator/User) are grounded in the **expertise-reversal effect** (Kalyuga
et al. 2003): agents with relevant prior domain expertise experience lower
effective cognitive load for security tasks, per Cognitive Load Theory
(Sweller, 1988). Per-agent parameters are randomly drawn from
role-level distributions, so the population trajectory is not hand-set —
see the script docstring for exact citations and parameter ranges.

### 3.2 Results vs manuscript claims

| Metric | Rebuilt ABM | Original paper claim |
|---|---|---|
| Overall UAI, week 0 | 0.284 | not stated (implied ~0.55 at some early point) |
| Overall UAI, week 12 | 0.853 | 0.82 |
| UAI range cited in manuscript | — | 0.55 → 0.82 |
| Experience gap, early | 0.265 | 0.16 |
| Experience gap, week 12 | 0.220 (17% relative narrowing) | 0.08 (50% relative narrowing) |
| Role ranking, week 12 | Security Analyst (0.952) > Sys Admin (0.908) > Operator (0.820) > User (0.733) | Security analysts highest, users lowest (matches direction) |

The final overall UAI (0.853) lands close to the manuscript's 0.82. The
**gap-narrowing is real but less dramatic** than claimed (17% vs 50%
relative reduction) — the model shows experienced/inexperienced roles
converging, just not as sharply. Statistical validation across 15 seeds:
final UAI = 0.851 ± 0.005, final gap = 0.220 ± 0.010 — both very stable
across runs, so this isn't noise.

## 4. Cross-layer synergy

The manuscript's Table 13 implies blockchain and human-factor layers
directly *raise AI detection accuracy* — there's no real mechanism for
that (detection accuracy is a property of the trained classifier; adding a
ledger or training users doesn't change what the model learned). Rather
than reproduce that framing, `results/synergy_index.py` builds an explicit,
disclosed composite **Security Effectiveness Index** (SEI = 0.5×detection +
0.25×availability + 0.25×user-adaptation, weights stated and open to
challenge):

| Scenario | SEI |
|---|---|
| AI only | 0.644 |
| AI + Blockchain | 0.644 |
| AI + Human Factor | 0.857 |
| Full framework (AI + Blockchain + Human Factor) | 0.857 |
| Full framework at week 0 (before adaptation) | 0.715 |

The honest "synergy" story: the full framework's advantage over AI-alone
comes almost entirely from the human-adaptation term (SEI jumps from 0.644
to 0.857 once user adaptation is included), while blockchain's own
contribution to this particular composite is small (0.644 → 0.644) because
its main benefit — availability — was already extremely high without it
(99.9% baseline). Blockchain's real value proposition in this framework is
better argued through auditability/tamper-resistance than through this
composite score.

## 5. What this means for the manuscript

Every core empirical claim in the Abstract, Results, and Conclusion is now
backed by a real, re-runnable computation — but the **numbers themselves
differ substantially** from what's currently written, in both directions
(some more conservative, some the model ceiling is lower than claimed,
gap-narrowing less dramatic, etc.). Before touching the manuscript text,
you'll want to decide:

1. **Accept the rebuilt numbers as the new ground truth** and rewrite the
   Abstract/Results/Discussion/Conclusion to match them (recommended if the
   original numbers can't be traced back to an actual prior experiment run
   with a defensible methodology).
2. **Investigate the blockchain throughput gap specifically** — if 420 TPS
   was genuinely measured on real infrastructure before, the sequential
   per-tx execution assumption here (5–20ms) is the parameter to revisit;
   tell me what the real per-tx execution cost was and I can recalibrate.
3. **Commission a small real human-rating study** (15–20 raters, a
   Likert-scale form comparing explanation clarity across the four XAI
   methods) to legitimately replace the 4.7/5 comprehensibility figure —
   this is a much smaller, faster undertaking than the full behavioural
   study that was discontinued, and I can build the rating instrument if
   useful.

I'd suggest we go through the manuscript section by section once you've
looked at these numbers, updating each claim to match what actually came
out of the rebuild.
